What it is

A pipeline and a set of dashboards for one question: what is going on in the Kansas cattle market, and how do hay prices and drought fit in? Every week, scripts pull the latest public reports from USDA Market News (the Kansas Weekly Cattle Auction Summary, the Kansas Direct Cattle report and the Kansas Direct Hay report) and from the U.S. Drought Monitor, clean them up and store them in a Postgres database. A few hours later the dashboards rebuild with the new week's numbers.

How it was built

The short version: scheduled scripts load four public reports into Postgres every Monday, a rebuild turns the database into a static dashboard site every Tuesday, and the site costs almost nothing to run. What's in it

  • Cattle Market Trends. The home page. Auction prices and volume over time: the latest week, a seasonal comparison by year, the heifer discount, price by weight class and feeder cattle volume. A class and a weight class dropdown (400 to 800 lb) drive the page.
  • Backgrounding Economics. A "sell now, or feed it up?" calculator. Set a steer's weight today, the weight to sell at, the gain per day and the hay it eats, and the page replays that choice in every past year with that year's auction and hay prices, to show what feeding added or took away.
  • Hay Market. What hay costs: price over time by hay type (alfalfa, brome, bluestem and wheat straw), by package (round or square bales) and by USDA quality grade.
  • Drought & Market Conditions. Kansas drought since 2000, and how it lines up with hay prices, steer prices and auction volume since 2020.
  • Direct vs. Auction. How cattle sold directly compare with the same cattle at the sale barn, by class and weight, and how much of the volume goes each way.

How to use it

  1. Open the dashboards and start with Cattle Market Trends for the current week.
  2. Pick a weight class or class where a page has a dropdown. The numbers and charts on that page follow it.
  3. Narrow the dates. Charts with a date filter let you jump to the last year, the last three months, or any range.
  4. Try the sliders on Backgrounding Economics: set the steer and the plan, and see what feeding it up would have added or cost in each past year. The gain per day and the hay it eats are my assumptions, not USDA data.
  5. Hover over a chart to see exact values. On the comparison panels the hover lines up across charts.
